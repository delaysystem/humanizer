In late 2022, researchers at the National Laboratory of California achieved a historic milestone in fusion energy. They produced more power from a fusion reaction than they delivered to fuel from lasers. This achievement, known as scientific ignition, represents the culmination of decades of research efforts. The result requires significant background: while the fusion reaction released more energy than the laser light that hit the target, the lasers consumed a lot of electricity from the grid. The actual energy gain, measured by wall-plug power, remains a distant goal. However, the demonstration proved that laboratory ignition is physically achievable, and subsequent experiments have improved the initial result. The journey from scientific demonstration to practical power generation involves major engineering challenges. A commercial facility would need to repeat the ignition process many times per second, compared to approximately one experiment per day currently. Currently, there is a need for mass production of fuel targets made individually at enormous cost. Laser systems occupying entire buildings will require a dramatic reduction in size and cost. Private companies pursuing alternative fusion methods have attracted considerable investment based on the premise that different designs can quickly overcome these obstacles. Most independent experts expect progress but warn that the timeline for grid-connected fusion energy is measured in decades, not years. Funding models reflect this long-term by providing consistent base support for public research budgets and venture capital chasing accelerated milestones. Workforce development has emerged as a silent constraint, with few specialized engineers and plasma physicists available to public laboratories and startups. University programs are expanding, but it is impossible to graduate talent overnight. International collaboration continues through large projects that pool resources across nations, even as competition intensifies over patents and technological leadership. Regulatory frameworks for fusion facilities remain largely unwritten, as existing nuclear regulations are designed around fission risks, which fusion does not share. Policymakers are beginning to formulate proportional oversight. Each incremental advance, from better magnets to improved fuel management, combines toward ultimate feasibility.
