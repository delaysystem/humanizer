Scientists are conducting open-field trials of wheat modified with CRISPR gene-editing technology, marking a significant milestone for agricultural biotechnology. Unlike traditional genetic modifications that introduce foreign DNA, these changes involve precise deletions within the plant's own genome. This difference has important regulatory implications, as many authorities classify such changes with conventional breeding methods. Initial results suggest that edited wheat significantly better resists fungal disease than conventional varieties, but does not affect yield. Researchers emphasize that multiple growing seasons will be needed to confirm these findings. This technology raises broader questions about the density of agricultural patents and the management of gene flow to wild relatives. Proponents argue that rapid crop improvement is essential.
