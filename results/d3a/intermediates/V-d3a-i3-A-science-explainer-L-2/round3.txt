In late 2022, researchers at the National Laboratory of California achieved a historic milestone in fusion energy. They produced more energy from the fusion reaction than they delivered to it from lasers. This achievement, known as scientific combustion, represents the culmination of decades of research efforts. The result requires significant context: while the fusion reaction released more energy than the laser light that hit the target, the lasers consumed a substantial amount of electricity from the grid. The actual energy gain, measured by wall-plug power, remains a distant goal. However, the demonstration proved that laboratory combustion is physically achievable, and subsequent experiments have improved the initial result. The journey from scientific demonstration to practical power generation involves major engineering challenges. A commercial facility would need to repeat the combustion process many times per second, compared to about one experiment per day currently. Currently, the cost of individually manufactured fuel targets accounts for a portion of the total cost of fuel targets. Laser systems occupying entire buildings require a dramatic reduction in size and cost. Private companies pursuing alternative fusion methods have attracted considerable investment based on the premise that different designs could quickly overcome these obstacles. Most independent experts anticipate progress but caution that the timeline for grid-connected fusion energy is measured in decades, not years. Funding models reflect this long-term perspective by providing consistent base support for public research budgets and venture capital that follows accelerated milestones. Workforce development has emerged as a silent constraint due to the limited supply of specialized engineers and plasma physicists required by public laboratories and startups. University programs are expanding, but it is impossible to graduate talent overnight. Despite increased competition over patents and technical leadership, international collaboration continues through large projects that pool resources across nations. Regulatory frameworks for fusion facilities are largely unwritten because existing nuclear regulations are designed around fission risks, which fusion does not share. Policymakers are beginning to formulate oversight of the ratio. Each incremental advance, from better magnets to improved fuel management, combines toward ultimate feasibility.
