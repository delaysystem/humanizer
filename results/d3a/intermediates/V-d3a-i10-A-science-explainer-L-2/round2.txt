In late 2022, researchers at the National Laboratory of California achieved a historic milestone in fusion energy. They produced more power from a fusion reaction than they delivered from lasers. This achievement, known as scientific combustion, represents the culmination of decades of research efforts. The result requires significant context: while the fusion reaction released more energy than the laser light that hit the target, the lasers consumed a substantial amount of electricity from the grid. The actual energy gain, measured by wall-plug power, remains a distant goal. However, the demonstration proved that laboratory combustion is physically achievable, and subsequent experiments have improved the initial result. The journey from scientific demonstration to practical power generation involves major engineering challenges. A commercial facility would need to repeat the combustion process many times per second, compared to about one experiment per day currently. Currently, the cost of fuel targets manufactured separately requires mass production of fuel targets. Laser systems occupying entire buildings require a dramatic reduction in size and cost. Private companies pursuing alternative fusion methods have attracted considerable investment based on the premise that different designs could quickly overcome these obstacles. Most independent experts anticipate progress but warn that the timeline for grid-connected fusion energy is measured in decades, not years. Funding models reflect this long-term outlook by providing consistent base support for public research budgets and venture capital that pursues accelerated milestones. Workforce development has emerged as a silent constraint due to the limited supply of specialized engineers and plasma physicists required by public laboratories and startups. University programs are expanding, but it is impossible to graduate talent overnight. International collaboration continues through large projects, which pool resources across nations, even as competition intensifies over patents and technological leadership. Regulatory frameworks for fusion facilities have largely been unwritten because existing nuclear regulations are designed around fission risks, which fusion does not share. Policymakers are beginning to formulate proportional oversight. Each incremental advancement, from better magnets to improved fuel management, combines toward ultimate feasibility.
