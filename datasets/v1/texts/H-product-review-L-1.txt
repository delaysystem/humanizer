I replaced our second car with the HaulMaster E-Cargo six months ago, and the odometer just passed 1,900 miles — nearly all of them trips the car used to make. School runs, grocery hauls, hardware-store lumber: the 400-pound capacity swallows loads that sound absurd until you try them. The dual-battery setup delivers a genuine 70 miles of assisted range in mixed riding, and the step-through frame means my teenager and I share it despite a ten-inch height gap. What surprised me most is how it changed our schedule. The school run takes twelve minutes by bike versus twenty-five by car in morning traffic, and parking at the farmers market went from a hunt to a non-event. The compromises are honest ones. At 78 pounds it is no joy to carry up steps, the stock saddle was terrible, and the $4,200 price needs the car-replacement math to make sense — ours pencils out in fourteen months of saved gas, insurance, and parking. Winter riding demanded studded tires ($180) and real rain gear. The dealer network is thin; my nearest certified shop is forty minutes away, though nothing has needed service yet beyond a brake adjustment I did myself. If your daily radius is under fifteen miles and your hills are moderate, an e-cargo bike is not a lifestyle statement. It is the most practical vehicle we own. The kid-hauling setup deserves its own mention: two seats with five-point harnesses plus a rain canopy ran $380 extra, and my six-year-old now refuses the car for school. Loading groceries takes practice — heavy items low and centered, or the steering gets vague. I learned that lesson with a watermelon and a wobble I would rather forget. Theft is the constant background worry; I run two locks totaling $240 and still choose parking carefully. The GPS tracker hidden in the seat tube ($50 plus a subscription) buys peace of mind. After 1,900 miles, brake pads are half-worn and the chain needs replacing — about $90 in parts I installed myself on a Saturday morning.
