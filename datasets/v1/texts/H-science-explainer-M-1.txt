The first CRISPR-edited wheat is now growing in open field trials across three states, and plant biologists are watching closely. Unlike earlier genetically modified crops, which carried genes spliced in from other species, this wheat has only small deletions in its own mildew-susceptibility genes — changes that could theoretically have arisen through mutation breeding, just far more slowly. The distinction matters legally: regulators in the United States treat such edits as conventional breeding, clearing a path to market without the decade-long reviews transgenic crops face. Europe is moving the same direction after years of deadlock. Early trial data show a 40 percent drop in fungal infection with no yield penalty, though researchers caution that one dry season proves little. Critics raise two objections worth taking seriously. First, edited traits can still cross into wild relatives through pollen, so containment rules for seed production need teeth. Second, the patents concentrate control of staple crops in a handful of firms. Both concerns are about governance, not the editing technique itself — a nuance often lost in the shouting.
