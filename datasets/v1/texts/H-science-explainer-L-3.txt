The idea sounds like folk medicine: transplanting gut microbes from a healthy person into a sick one. But fecal microbiota transplants have cured recurrent Clostridioides difficile infections — a gut scourge that kills 15,000 Americans a year — with success rates near 90 percent, far above antibiotics. Regulators approved the first standardized products in 2023, replacing the improvised donor screening of the early years. Now researchers are testing whether the same principle extends to conditions where the microbiome's role is murkier: ulcerative colitis, irritable bowel syndrome, even Parkinson's and depression, where gut-brain signaling is an active frontier. Results here are humbler and harder to read. Colitis trials show real but partial responses — perhaps a third of patients improve significantly — and effects fade unless transplants are repeated. The field's central frustration is ignorance: a transplanted community contains hundreds of species, and nobody knows which ones do the work. That is changing as teams isolate defined bacterial cocktails, some now in late-stage trials, which would turn a crude transplant into a manufactured drug with consistent dosing. Skeptics warn the hype has outrun the data before — probiotic marketing spent a decade promising what only transplants delivered. The next five years of controlled trials, preregistered and placebo-controlled in ways the early work was not, will show whether the microbiome is medicine's next antibiotic or its next cold fusion. The regulatory path reveals how hard this judgment is. Agencies approved the first products for recurrent C. difficile — where the evidence is overwhelming — while requiring full drug-style trials for every new indication. That split frustrates patients with colitis who read the headlines and want access now, but the history of medicine is littered with plausible treatments that failed controlled testing. Donor screening has quietly become one of the strictest in medicine: candidates are rejected for everything from recent travel to high BMI, and fewer than three percent qualify. Meanwhile the do-it-yourself community the regulators hoped to retire persists online, trading protocols of uneven safety. The responsible middle ground — expanded trial access, honest timelines — satisfies nobody completely, which is usually a sign it is roughly right.
