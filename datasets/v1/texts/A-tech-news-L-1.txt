A contentious debate over the accuracy of the state's official broadband coverage map continues to escalate, with hundreds of millions of dollars in federal funding hanging in the balance. The map, which is compiled from data self-reported by internet service providers, indicates that the vast majority of addresses have access to high-speed service. However, a coalition of rural counties disputes these figures, presenting tens of thousands of crowd-sourced speed tests that suggest coverage is significantly overstated. The discrepancy stems largely from the mapping methodology, under which an entire census block is classified as served if even a single location within it has access. Both critics and providers acknowledge this approach is flawed, though they disagree sharply on the true extent of coverage. The outcome matters enormously because federal infrastructure grants will be distributed based on the map's findings. Communities incorrectly marked as served could miss out on funding entirely. Providers argue that volunteer-collected data is unreliable and point to their own measurements showing adequate service. Meanwhile, residents in disputed areas describe daily struggles with unreliable connections that undermine remote work and education. The legislature mandated a new mapping process two years ago, but implementation has been plagued by delays and contractor changes. A court hearing scheduled for January could reshape the funding landscape. Federal officials have cautioned that grants may be reconsidered if the underlying data proves materially inaccurate. Technical experts recommend address-level mapping as the only reliable path forward, though providers argue the cost is prohibitive. Several states have begun independent verification programs using their own testing infrastructure. These efforts face methodological debates about representative sampling and measurement standards. Consumer advocacy groups continue collecting crowd-sourced data to supplement official figures. The controversy has attracted national attention as other states confront similar discrepancies in their own coverage claims.
