The seasonal flu shot is a yearly educated guess, and some years the guess misses badly. Each February, global health agencies predict which influenza strains will dominate the following winter, manufacturers grow the chosen strains in chicken eggs for six months, and by autumn the match is sometimes poor — efficacy has dipped as low as 19 percent in bad years. Messenger-RNA technology promises to break this cycle on two fronts. First, mRNA shots can be manufactured in weeks rather than months, so the strain decision can wait until much closer to flu season, when forecasts are sharper. Second, researchers can encode pieces of several strains, or even conserved parts of the virus that barely change year to year, raising hopes for broader protection. The clinical results so far are mixed in instructive ways. A large 2024 trial of an mRNA flu vaccine showed efficacy comparable to standard shots against matched strains but notably stronger antibody responses in adults over 65, whose immune systems respond weakly to egg-grown vaccines. Side effects skewed higher, though: a day of fatigue and sore arms was more common than with the traditional shot. The deeper question is whether mRNA can deliver the long-sought universal flu vaccine. Early-phase trials targeting the virus's conserved stem region produced durable antibodies in most volunteers, but nobody knows yet whether those antibodies actually prevent infection — the efficacy trial now enrolling 20,000 participants should answer that by 2027. Cost is the quiet obstacle. At current prices, mRNA doses run several times the cost of egg-based shots, a gap that matters enormously for the global south, where flu kills hundreds of thousands yearly. Manufacturers insist scale will close it. If the efficacy data hold, the annual guessing game that has defined flu prevention for eighty years may finally be winding down.
