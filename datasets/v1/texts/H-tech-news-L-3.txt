Negotiators for the port authority and the dockworkers' union returned to the table Monday with a federal mediator and a deadline: the current contract expires in eleven days, and a strike would idle the country's third-busiest container terminal. The central dispute is automation. Management wants to install semi-automated cranes on two additional berths, promising that no current employee will be laid off. The union calls that promise hollow, noting that retirements are simply not being backfilled — membership has fallen 15 percent in five years even as cargo volume hit records. Behind the jobs fight sits a productivity argument with real numbers attached. Turnaround times at the port's one automated berth average 31 hours per vessel, versus 52 hours at conventional berths, and shipping lines have begun routing overflow cargo to a rival port 200 miles south. Union president Angela Ruiz concedes the gap but argues the automated berth's figures exclude the cost of its frequent software outages, including a nine-hour stoppage in August that stranded two ships. Economists watching the talks say a strike of even a week would ripple through holiday supply chains, particularly electronics and auto parts. Both sides have agreed to a media blackout, a sign, veterans say, that serious bargaining has finally begun. What the public numbers miss is the pension math quietly driving the union's position. The fund covers 4,200 retirees and assumes seven percent annual returns it has not achieved in four years; every unfilled position means one fewer contributor. Management's actuaries privately concede the point but argue automation revenue could backstop the fund — a promise the union wants in binding language, not press releases. The generational split inside the hall is real: younger members packing the overflow room care more about starting pay and overtime rules than about crane semantics. Ruiz acknowledged the tension in her remarks, promising no deal trades the future for the present. Retired dockworker Frank Delgado, watching from the back at 78, put it more bluntly: every contract since 1989 has been about the machines, and the machines keep winning slowly.
