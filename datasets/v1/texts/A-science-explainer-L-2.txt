In late 2022, researchers at a California national laboratory achieved a historic milestone in fusion energy, producing more energy from a fusion reaction than was delivered to the fuel by lasers. This achievement, known as scientific ignition, represented the culmination of decades of research effort. The result requires important context: while the fusion reaction released more energy than the laser light striking the target, the lasers themselves consumed vastly more electricity from the grid. True energy gain measured from wall-plug power remains a distant goal. Nevertheless, the demonstration proved that laboratory ignition is physically achievable, and subsequent experiments have improved upon the initial result. The journey from scientific demonstration to practical power generation involves formidable engineering challenges. A commercial facility would need to repeat the ignition process many times per second, compared to roughly one experiment per day currently. Fuel targets that are presently manufactured individually at enormous cost would need mass production at negligible prices. Laser systems occupying entire buildings would require dramatic reductions in size and expense. Private companies pursuing alternative fusion approaches have attracted substantial investment based on the premise that different designs can overcome these obstacles more quickly. Most independent experts expect progress but caution that timelines to grid-connected fusion power are measured in decades rather than years. Funding patterns reflect this long horizon, with public research budgets providing steady baseline support while venture capital chases accelerated milestones. Workforce development has emerged as a quiet constraint, as the specialized engineers and plasma physicists needed by both public labs and startups are in short supply. University programs are expanding but cannot graduate talent overnight. International collaboration continues through large projects that pool resources across nations, even as competition intensifies over patents and technical leadership. Regulatory frameworks for fusion facilities remain largely unwritten, since existing nuclear rules were designed around fission risks that fusion does not share. Policymakers are beginning to craft proportionate oversight. Each incremental advance, from better magnets to improved fuel handling, compounds toward eventual viability.
