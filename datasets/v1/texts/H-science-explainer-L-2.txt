In December 2022, a facility in California did something no laboratory had ever done: it got more energy out of a fusion reaction than the lasers put in. The margin was thin — about 3 megajoules out versus 2 in — and the fine print was thick. Those 2 megajoules were only the light striking the fuel pellet; the lasers themselves drew roughly 400 megajoules from the grid. By any honest wall-plug accounting, the experiment consumed a hundred times what it produced. Yet physicists celebrated, because the result crossed a symbolic threshold called ignition that had resisted fifty years of attempts. Since then, the lab has repeated ignition several times with steadily better yields, most recently above 5 megajoules. The path from here to a power plant, however, runs through problems physics alone cannot solve. A commercial plant must ignite pellets about ten times per second; the record-setting facility manages roughly one shot per day. Each target — a peppercorn-sized diamond shell holding frozen hydrogen — is handmade at a cost of thousands of dollars; a power plant would need millions per year at pennies apiece. And the laser hall, three football fields of optics, would have to shrink and cheapen by orders of magnitude. Private fusion companies claim they will sidestep these economics with different designs — compact magnets, alternative fuels — and investors have poured billions into the bet. The sober view from most academic researchers: ignition proved the science, but the engineering staircase to grid power has dozens of steps, and funding currently covers the first few.
