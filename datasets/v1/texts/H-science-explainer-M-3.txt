Microplastics have been found in rainwater on every continent, including Antarctica. The particles — mostly fibers shed from synthetic clothing and fragments of degraded packaging — ride wind currents for thousands of miles before washing out of the sky. A 2024 survey of 30 cities measured an average of 120 particles per liter of rainfall, with the highest counts downwind of industrial zones. What this means for health is genuinely uncertain. Laboratory studies show that very high doses inflame lung and gut tissue in mice, but nobody drinks enough rain to match those exposures, and the particles in rain are chemically diverse — some inert, some carrying additives like flame retardants. Researchers stress two priorities: standardizing measurement, since current studies count different size ranges and cannot be compared, and tracing which sources dominate, because fibers from laundry imply very different policies than fragments from tire wear. Until then, the honest summary is that exposure is universal and consequences are unknown.
