Vaccines work by giving the immune system a practice run. A killed virus, a weakened one, or just a fragment of it is introduced so that B cells learn to make matching antibodies without the danger of a real infection. Most of those fighters die off afterward, but a small pool of memory cells remains on standby for years. If the actual pathogen ever shows up, the memory cells recognize it immediately and flood the bloodstream with antibodies before the invader can establish itself. This is why vaccinated people often never feel sick at all — the battle is over before symptoms begin. Booster shots exist because some memory pools fade, and a reminder restores them.
