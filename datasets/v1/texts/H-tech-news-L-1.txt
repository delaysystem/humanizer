The fight over the state's official broadband map entered its third year this month, and the stakes keep rising. At issue is a deceptively simple question: which addresses can actually buy high-speed internet? The map, assembled from provider self-reports, shows 94 percent coverage. A coalition of rural counties says the real number is closer to 70, and it has the speed tests to prove it — 40,000 of them, collected by volunteers over eighteen months. The gap matters because $380 million in federal grants will be allocated strictly by the map. Under the current version, dozens of underserved townships would receive nothing. Provider groups counter that the volunteer tests are unscientific, taken on old routers over congested Wi-Fi, and they point to their own drive-test data showing strong coverage along highways. Both sides agree on one fact: the mapping methodology, which counts an entire census block as served if a single home in it can get service, is indefensible. The legislature ordered a fix in 2024, but the contracted remapping firm missed two deadlines and had its contract rebid. Meanwhile, residents like Joanne Pike of Cedar Falls, who runs a bookkeeping business over a connection that drops every afternoon, have stopped following the proceedings. Her township is marked served because a fiber line passes the highway a mile from her house. A judge will hear the coalition's challenge in January, and federal officials have warned that money could be clawed back if the map is found materially wrong. The mapping contractor's failure deserves scrutiny of its own. Awarded $2.1 million in 2024, the firm delivered a methodology memo eight months late, then asked for another year after its pilot county produced numbers the state called unusable. The rebid drew only two offers, a sign of how specialized — and thankless — the work has become. County IT directors, meanwhile, have built their own stopgap maps from permit records and complaint logs. Three counties now publish theirs openly, and embarrassingly for the state, grant applicants cite the county maps as more trustworthy. Whatever the judge decides in January, the era of provider self-reporting as the sole source of truth appears finished.
