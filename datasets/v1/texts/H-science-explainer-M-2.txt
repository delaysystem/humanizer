Earthquake early warning sounds impossible — how do you outrun a rupture? The trick is that quakes broadcast their arrival. When a fault slips, it releases fast, weak P-waves and slower, destructive S-waves. Seismometers near the epicenter feel the P-waves first, and computers estimate the magnitude within seconds, firing alerts to cities the S-waves have not reached yet. Warnings range from a few seconds to a minute depending on distance: enough to stop trains, open elevator doors, and duck under desks. Japan's system, the most mature in the world, has issued over a hundred public alerts since 2007. California's ShakeAlert now pushes to every phone in the state. The systems are not perfect. In 2022 a faulty sensor triggered a false alarm in Tokyo during a morning commute, and magnitude estimates for very large quakes start too low because the rupture is still growing while the algorithm decides. Engineers accept these flaws as the price of speed — a system that waits for certainty warns no one.
